\section{The scaling challenge}
\label{sec:mono-intro-scaling}

For five decades the density and speed of integrated circuits grew by shrinking the transistor. That route has become
expensive in three ways. Physically, the gains of geometric scaling have slowed since the end of constant-field
(Dennard) scaling, when supply voltages and clock frequencies stopped falling and rising together
\citep{Theis2017}; further performance must increasingly come from architecture, software and integration
\citep{Leiserson2020,Salahuddin2018}. Industrially, keeping electrostatic control at short gate lengths has driven the
move from planar devices to fins and to stacked nanosheets beyond the 3~nm node, and conventional semiconductors lose
performance rapidly when their channels are thinned, below about 3~nm for silicon and 10~nm for InGaAs
\citep[p.~164]{Si2022}. Energetically, the electricity used by computing infrastructure has become a measurable share of
global consumption; one bottom-up estimate puts data centres at about 1\,\% of global electricity use in 2018
\citep{Masanet2020}. Each further gain in logic or memory density therefore has to be bought with new device concepts
rather than with the next lithography step alone.

\paragraph{Monolithic three-dimensional integration.} One such concept is to build additional layers of transistors
above the copper interconnect of a finished chip, for example as memory or select devices close to logic. Such
back-end-of-line (BEOL) devices must be processed at temperatures that the interconnect survives, below about 400~\textdegree C
\citep[p.~1]{Januar2026}, which excludes crystalline silicon and makes oxide semiconductors the leading candidates
\citep{Datta2019,Salahuddin2018}. Amorphous oxide semiconductors were developed for display transistors
\citep{Nomura2004,Kamiya2010,Fortunato2012}; their extremely low off-currents have since been exploited in
capacitor-less DRAM cells with retention times beyond 400~s \citep{Belmonte2020}.

\paragraph{Indium oxide at the thickness limit.} Indium oxide has a much higher mobility than the gallium- and
zinc-containing amorphous oxides, but it is too conductive to switch off unless the channel is very thin. Atomic-layer
deposited amorphous \InO{} channels as thin as 0.7~nm give enhancement-mode transistors whose threshold voltage and
carrier density are tuned by the channel thickness, an effect attributed to quantum confinement shifting the Fermi level
relative to the conduction band \citep[p.~500]{Si2021}; nanometre-thick \InO{} transistors reach drain currents above
10~A/mm \citep[p.~21536]{Lin2022}. Doping with W is one way to control the carrier density and stability of such
channels; its effect on stability depends on thickness \citep{Kim2024}.

\paragraph{The cost of thinning.} Making the channel a few nanometres thick changes the material itself. The band gap
opens and the band edges move by quantum confinement; every atom is close to a surface or an interface; the films are
disordered; and transport becomes, in the words of the device paper's introduction, disorder-limited below about 10~nm
for conventional amorphous oxides \citep[p.~1]{Januar2026}. Variability between nominally identical devices grows when a
channel contains only a few hundred donors per square micrometre (a 2~nm channel at the calibrated effective donor
density of $2.5\times10^{17}$\cmcu{} holds about 500, \claim{C016}). Understanding these effects requires simulations at
two very different scales, and both are costly: a first-principles calculation scales as the cube of the number of
atoms (\cref{sec:mono-dft-cost}; \citealp{Goedecker1999}), and amorphous films need hundreds of atoms per cell and
several independent samples; a device simulation must resolve nanometre-thick layers in micrometre-long devices, and
each calibration run is a separate launch. The computational cost of this project is documented in
\cref{app:mono-resources}.

\section{The research problem}
\label{sec:mono-intro-problem}

The fabrication group of this project has published a thickness series of W-doped \InO{} (IWO, about 2\,\% W)
bottom-gate transistors with channels of 2.0 to 13.2~nm, together with an analytic model in which the current is a blend
of band, tail-state and interface-trap terms, eight parameters fitted per device, and crystalline density-functional
calculations of W-doped \InO{} \citep{Januar2026} (the W fraction of those cells is not given in the main text
available to this project). The paper reports that W passivates oxygen vacancies and promotes charge
delocalisation \citep[p.~4]{Januar2026}, and that a single model captures the crossover from near-band transport in
thicker channels to disorder- and interface-limited transport in ultrathin films \citep[p.~4]{Januar2026}.

Three questions remain open after that work, and they define the problem of this monograph:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Can a device model with one parameter set shared by all thicknesses, with inputs computed from first principles
rather than fitted per device, account for the measured thickness dependence? The project's earlier per-film models
reproduce each curve, but every attempt to predict the 6.3~nm device from the others failed (\claim{C014}).
\item What does confinement actually do to the electronic structure of \InO{} films of one to two nanometres, how is the
gap opening shared between the bands, and how much of it reaches the device threshold?
\item What does a W atom do in a sub-nanometre amorphous film: does it still give its electron to extended states, or
does dimensionality change its activity?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Approach and scope of this monograph}
\label{sec:mono-intro-approach}

The project combines (i) a registered extraction of the published devices' transfer curves, (ii) drift--diffusion device
simulations from per-film calibrations to a shared-parameter campaign, (iii) density-functional calculations of crystalline
\InO{} films from 0.46 to 1.96~nm, of W in crystalline cells and of oxygen vacancies, and (iv) a registered campaign on
amorphous films from 0.7 to 2.0~nm. Every test was registered before its outcome where that was possible, and every
statement carries a provenance label (\cref{ch:mono-questions}).

This monograph is a versioned derivative of the project's full report of September 2026. It reuses the chapters of that
report that are still correct, revises those that the later evidence corrected, and adds the evidence of 26 September to
14 October 2026: the first-principles theory and its numerical checks, the masses and device parameters, tungsten and
oxygen vacancies, the amorphous protocol, the shared-model campaign, the provenance of the published metrics, the
multiscale synthesis, alternative explanations and an audit of publication constraints.

\paragraph{What this monograph does not contain.} No electronic result of any amorphous film and no outcome of the
device-simulation campaign from stage S2 on: both are sealed under the project's blind-analysis rules at the evidence
state of this document, and appear only as placeholders. No new measurement exists. No claim in this document is approved
for publication.

\paragraph{Contributions that stand on the present evidence.} (1) A first-principles confinement series of crystalline
\InO{} films, with masses on a stated basis, a band-edge partition, a hybrid-functional check, and laws with honest
uncertainties (\cref{ch:dft,ch:mono-masses}). (2) The finding that the mass increase of the films is consistent, without fitting,
with the bulk non-parabolicity at the computed subband energy (within 1\,\% at 2~nm and 10\,\% at 1~nm), so that the
device model carries, to that accuracy, one independent first-principles law (\cref{ch:mono-confinement-theory}). (3) A corrected picture of W in crystalline
\InO{} as a resonant donor at high filling, with the earlier deep-donor reading withdrawn (\cref{ch:mono-w-vac}).
(4) A registered crystalline vacancy test (\cref{ch:mono-w-vac}). (5) A fully specified, blind protocol for amorphous
films, including the finding that its central hypothesis cannot return a crossover verdict (\cref{ch:mono-amorph}).
(6) A quantitative description of how every failed 6.3~nm prediction relates to the computed confinement shift, all in
the same direction, without assigning a cause (\cref{ch:mono-multiscale}).
(7) A reconciliation of the published and the derived device metrics (\cref{ch:mono-provenance}).

\section{Structure}
\label{sec:mono-intro-structure}

Part~I states the problem, the questions and the literature basis. Part~II gives the theory: electronic structure, confinement,
defects and localisation, and the device electrostatics and transport derivations of the earlier report. Part~III treats
the device data and the device simulations, from calibration to the shared-model campaign. Part~IV reports the
first-principles results. Part~V synthesises the multiscale link, the alternative explanations, the publication audit and
the conclusions. The appendices hold the run catalogue, code and decks of the earlier report, the evidence registers, the
corrections register and the computational resources.

\begin{keyresult}
Oxide semiconductors are candidates for transistors built above the interconnect of a chip, where only low process
temperatures are allowed, and indium oxide is attractive there only as a film a few nanometres thick. Thinning changes the
material: confinement, surfaces and disorder dominate. The published IWO thickness series was described by a per-device
fitted model; this monograph asks whether a shared model with computed inputs can account for it, what confinement does
to the electronic structure, and what W does in sub-nanometre amorphous films, and it reports what the evidence available
on 14 October 2026 can and cannot answer.
\end{keyresult}
