\chapter{Donors, defects and localisation: theory of the computed observables}
\label{ch:mono-defect-theory}

The first-principles questions of this project about tungsten and oxygen vacancies are questions about donors: does an
impurity give its electron to an extended band, at what energy, and how does that change in a thin film? This chapter
states the theory needed to read the computed observables (level positions, projected occupations, participation
ratios, vacancy energy differences) and the limits of what neutral, highly doped supercells can say about donors at
device densities.

% =====================================================================================================================
\section{Shallow donors and the dilute limit}
\label{sec:mono-defect-hydrogenic}

A donor whose extra electron is bound only by the screened Coulomb potential of its ionised core is described by the
effective-mass equation with the potential $-e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_s r)$. Its ground state is hydrogen-like
with
\begin{equation}
E_D = \frac{m^*/m_0}{\varepsilon_s^2}\,\mathrm{Ry}, \qquad a_B^* = \frac{\varepsilon_s}{m^*/m_0}\,a_0 ,
\label{eq:mono-defect-hyd}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{Ry} = 13.606$~eV and $a_0 = 0.0529$~nm. For \InO{} with the measured $m^* = 0.208\,m_0$ and static
permittivity $\varepsilon_s = 10.55$ \citep[p.~225102-1]{Stokey2021}, $E_D = 25$~meV and $a_B^* = 2.7$~nm; with the PBE
band-edge mass, 19~meV and 3.6~nm. The Mott criterion $n_c^{1/3}a_B^* \approx 0.26$ gives a critical density of about
$9\times10^{17}$\cmcu{}. Above it, donor states overlap into the conduction band and the material is degenerate. The
device channels operate below and around this density; every W-doped supercell of this project (one electron per W in
the amorphous cells, about $6\times10^{20}$\cmcu{}; three per W in the crystalline cells, $1.4$--$3.0\times10^{21}$\cmcu{})
lies about three orders of magnitude above it (some 660 times for the amorphous cells, 1500--3300 times for the
crystalline cells). In those cells a bound hydrogenic donor state cannot exist: the donated
electrons fill the band and screen each other. Any localisation the supercells show is therefore of a non-hydrogenic
kind (a d level, a trap, or a disorder tail state), and the supercells cannot test hydrogenic binding at device
densities.

\paragraph{Thin films: quantum and dielectric confinement.} When the film is thinner than $a_B^*$, the donor wave
function is squeezed and the binding grows; in the strict two-dimensional limit the hydrogenic binding energy is four
times the bulk value, 0.10~eV with the measured parameters, with a radius $a_B^*/2 = 1.3$~nm \citep{Bastard1981b}.
In addition, the field lines of the donor charge leave a thin film into its surroundings. If those have a lower
permittivity than the film, screening at distances beyond about $r_0 = \varepsilon_s t/(\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2)$ (the thin-film screening
length, \citealp{Cudazzo2011})
is set by the mean permittivity of the surroundings rather than by $\varepsilon_s$. In the devices the channel lies
on 2~nm of \AlO{} ($\varepsilon\approx9$) on 15~nm of \HfO{} ($\varepsilon = 19.57$), with air above
(\cref{ch:device}). For an \AlO{}/air environment the mean is 5, and the strict 2-D binding computed with it is about
0.45~eV; for an \HfO{}/air environment the mean is 10.3, close to $\varepsilon_s$, and there is essentially no
dielectric enhancement (0.11~eV, the quantum-confinement value). Because the \AlO{} layer (2~nm) is about as thick as
$r_0 \approx 2.1$~nm and the 2-D donor radius (1.3~nm), the device lies between these cases, closer to the second at
large distances. For $t = 2$~nm, about $0.75\,a_B^*$, quantum confinement alone roughly doubles the bulk binding. These
are order-of-magnitude estimates: at binding energies above the LO-phonon energy the static $\varepsilon_s$ overstates
the screening, and non-parabolicity raises the mass, both of which would increase the binding. Donor deactivation by dielectric confinement has been predicted and observed in
silicon nanowires \citep{Diarra2007,Bjork2009}. For the IWO channels this gives a mechanism by which the fraction of
ionised donors could fall with thickness without any change of W chemistry (\prov{theory}; not computed in this
project). It acts in the same direction as the fitted donor law of the device model (\claim{C016}), and it competes
with the W-specific mechanisms that H-N was designed to probe (\cref{sec:mono-am-why}).

\section{Resonant donors}
\label{sec:mono-defect-resonant}

A donor whose localised level lies inside the conduction band hybridises with the band states and becomes a resonance
of finite width. Its electron is not bound: it decays into the band, and the impurity is ionised whatever the
temperature. A resonant level becomes occupied only when the Fermi level reaches it; at that point the large density of
states of the resonance pins $E_F$ and sets a doping limit. This picture has been used to explain why Mo donors in \InO{} give high mobilities: the ionised impurity scatters
less when its d level lies well above the band edge \citep{Swallow2020}; for W the same argument applies by analogy.

The computed W cells of this project (\cref{ch:mono-w-vac}) are consistent with this picture in one respect and silent
in another. The W-5d-rich level lies 1.2--1.8~eV above the host band bottom at the filling of the cells, and in every
cell it lies within about 0.1~eV of $E_F$ \claim{C022}: at the cells' forced filling ($1.4$--$3.0\times10^{21}$\cmcu{}) the Fermi
level has reached the resonance. This coincidence is not independent evidence of pinning (a partly occupied narrow level
lies at $E_F$ by construction, and 0.1~eV is below the 0.136~eV smearing width), and whether the resonance pins $E_F$ at
lower filling would need a density series. They say nothing directly about lower fillings, because no lower-density cell was computed, and a hybrid
functional, which can lower localised d levels, was not applied to them. That W remains an ionised resonant donor at
device densities is therefore an inference from the computed level positions, not a computed result.

\section{Formation energies, charge states and what neutral cells can show}
\label{sec:mono-defect-formation}

The thermodynamic description of a defect $X$ in charge state $q$ uses its formation energy
\citep{VandeWalle2004,Freysoldt2014}
\begin{equation}
E^f[X^q](E_F) = E_{\mathrm{tot}}[X^q] - E_{\mathrm{tot}}[\mathrm{host}] - \sum_i n_i\mu_i + q\,(E_F + E_{\mathrm{VBM}})
 + E_{\mathrm{corr}}[X^q] ,
\label{eq:mono-defect-ef}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ atoms of chemical potential $\mu_i$ are added ($n_i>0$) or removed, $E_F$ is measured from the
valence-band maximum, and $E_{\mathrm{corr}}$ removes the spurious interaction of a charged defect with its periodic
images and aligns the potentials. The charge-transition level $\varepsilon(q/q')$ is the Fermi level at which the
formation energies of $q$ and $q'$ are equal,
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(q/q') = \frac{E^f[X^q](E_F{=}0) - E^f[X^{q'}](E_F{=}0)}{q'-q} .
\label{eq:mono-defect-ctl}
\end{equation}
A donor is shallow if $\varepsilon(+/0)$ lies within a few $\kT$ of the conduction band or above it. Computing this
requires charged cells, image-charge corrections \citep{Freysoldt2014}, a band-gap correction (semi-local functionals
place the conduction band too low, \citealp{Lany2008}) and, for localised states, a functional that does not
over-delocalise. For the oxygen vacancy in \InO{} the answer depends on these choices, and published first-principles
studies with different functionals and corrections disagree on how deep it is
\citep{Lany2007,Agoston2009,Buckeridge2018,Chatratin2019}.

\paragraph{What the neutral vacancy cells of this project compute.} The registered test H-L (\cref{ch:mono-w-vac}) uses
neutral, spin-unpolarised PBE cells with metallic filling. It defines energy differences in which the chemical
potentials cancel by construction, for example
\begin{equation}
\Delta E_{\mathrm{dop}} = \big(E[\mathrm{In_{31}WO_{47}}] - E[\mathrm{In_{31}WO_{48}}]\big) - \big(E[\mathrm{In_{32}O_{47}}] - E[\mathrm{In_{32}O_{48}}]\big),
\label{eq:mono-defect-ddop}
\end{equation}
the change of the neutral-vacancy energy caused by W. In neutral cells the electrons released by the vacancy go into the
conduction band at the Fermi level set by the cell. In the W cell that band is already filled by the three W electrons
to 1.7~eV above its bottom, so \cref{eq:mono-defect-ddop} contains a band-filling contribution as well as local
chemistry, strain and image interactions. It is a well-defined computed number, but not a formation-energy difference
at any device Fermi level, and not an upper or lower bound on one \claim{C038}.

\section{Polarons and self-trapping}
\label{sec:mono-defect-polaron}

A carrier can lower its energy by distorting the lattice around itself and becoming localised (a small polaron). Whether
this happens depends on the balance between the band-width energy lost on localisation and the lattice energy gained,
and semi-local functionals are biased against it because of their delocalisation error
(\cref{sec:mono-dft-xc}). Hybrid-functional work predicts that holes self-trap in \InO{} \citep{Varley2012}; the
electrons of the dispersive In-5s conduction band do not. For this project two consequences follow. The valence
effective density of states computed in \cref{ch:mono-masses} is a free-hole band integral and does not describe hole
transport or equilibrium hole populations \claim{C040}. And an electron localised on W in a reduced, W-5d-like state
would be a polaron-like object that PBE without $+U$ disfavours, so a PBE result that finds W donating into extended
states is weaker evidence than a PBE result that finds localisation (\cref{sec:mono-am-scope}).

\section{Projections onto atoms}
\label{sec:mono-defect-lowdin}

The atom weights of a Kohn--Sham state are obtained by projecting it onto atomic orbitals $\{\phi_\mu\}$. Because these
overlap, they are first orthonormalised symmetrically, $\tilde\phi_\mu = \sum_\nu (S^{-1/2})_{\nu\mu}\phi_\nu$ with
$S_{\mu\nu} = \langle\phi_\mu|\phi_\nu\rangle$ \citep{Lowdin1950}, which keeps each orthonormal orbital as close as
possible to its parent. The weight of atom $i$ in state $\psi$ is $\tilde p_i = \sum_{\mu\in i}|\langle\tilde\phi_\mu|\psi\rangle|^2$.
The atomic basis does not span the plane-wave space, so $\sum_i\tilde p_i = 1-s$ with the spilling $s$
\citep{SanchezPortal1995}; states with large spilling (for example states with weight in the vacuum or between atoms)
are poorly described, which is why the register flags completeness below 0.90. Three limits of these weights matter
here. They depend on the choice of atomic orbitals. They are weights of single states, and a W-5d ``count'' obtained by
summing them over occupied states, weighted by smearing occupations, is not the W charge (\corr{X11}). And the
projection of a degenerate or nearly degenerate set of states is meaningful only for the set, not for individual band
indices, which is why the crystalline analysis reads band characters at $\Gamma$ and why ordering bands by energy at each
$k$ can mix host and W character.

\section{Localisation measures}
\label{sec:mono-defect-pr}

For normalised atom weights $p_i$ ($\sum_i p_i = 1$) of a state in a cell of $N$ atoms, the inverse participation ratio
$\mathrm{IPR} = \sum_ip_i^2$ and the normalised participation ratio
\begin{equation}
\PRn = \frac{1}{N\sum_i p_i^2}
\label{eq:mono-defect-pr}
\end{equation}
\citep{BellDean1970,Thouless1974} measure the fraction of atoms the state occupies: a state with equal weight on $M$
atoms and none elsewhere has $\PRn = M/N$, so $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = N\,\PRn = M$. A state spread over the whole cell has
$\PRn \to 1$; a state on one atom has $\PRn = 1/N$. Unequal weights lower $\PRn$: a state with half its weight on W and
the other half spread evenly over the six first-shell O has $\sum p_i^2 = 0.25 + 6/144$ and $N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.4$.

\paragraph{A property of absolute thresholds across a cell series.} The register classifies states with absolute
thresholds, extended if $\PRn \ge 0.20$ and localised if $\PRn \le 0.05$ (\cref{sec:mono-am-defs}). Because $\PRn$ of a
state with fixed support scales as $1/N$, the same localised support is classified differently in cells of different
size. \Cref{tab:mono-defect-pr} lists what the thresholds mean for the registered W cells (atom counts from the
register and the ledger; no electronic quantity involved). A state spread evenly over W and its six first-shell O atoms
($M = 7$) would count as localised in the 0.7, 0.8 and 2.0~nm cells but as intermediate in the bulk and 1.0~nm cells;
more concentrated states (as the example above) are localised in all of them. The register acknowledges the $1/N$
scaling and requires $N_{\mathrm{eff}}$ to be reported with every state; the table is a reading aid for the released
per-cell results, not a change of the frozen rule (\prov{derived}).

\paragraph{Localisation in disordered systems.} In an infinite disordered system, states below a mobility edge are
localised and states above it extended \citep{Anderson1958,Mott1969}; in two dimensions weak disorder already localises
all states on long length scales \citep{Abrahams1979}. A supercell of 1--3~nm lateral size cannot resolve localisation
lengths larger than itself: a state that is localised on a scale of tens of nanometres appears extended in the cell.
The H-N classification therefore detects only strong localisation, on the scale of a few atoms, which is the scale
on which a deep, d-like W-centred trap would act; shallow traps of nanometre extent are invisible to it.

\section{Compensation and the effective donor density of the devices}
\label{sec:mono-defect-compensation}

At about 2\,\% W (about $5.8\times10^{20}$ W per cm$^3$ if the 2\,\% is a cation fraction), complete activation would
give between about $6\times10^{20}$ (one electron per W, if each W brings one extra O, as in the amorphous cells) and
$1.8\times10^{21}$ electrons per cm$^3$ (three per W, as in the crystalline cells), whereas the
calibrated donor law gives effective densities of $2.5\times10^{17}$ (thinnest films) to $1.8\times10^{18}$\cmcu{} (31.8~nm; \claim{C016}),
and the 2~nm device switches off. If the fitted density were a physical donor density, the difference of two to four orders of magnitude would have
to come from compensation (acceptor-like defects such as excess oxygen, cation vacancies or surface and interface
acceptor states), from donors that are not activated, or from both \citep{Lany2007}. The fitted value is not
identifiable from transfer curves (\claim{C016}), however: it also absorbs interface charge, trap densities and
work-function errors of the model, so the comparison is an argument of scale, not a measurement of compensation
(\prov{inferred}). A first-principles calculation of one W atom in a neutral cell
addresses only the second possibility, and only in the degenerate regime. This is why the device model treats the
donor density as a fitted, film-specific parameter (\claim{C016}), and why the multiscale chain of this project does
not pass a donor density from first principles to the device model (\cref{ch:mono-multiscale}).

\begin{keyresult}
The supercells of this project are degenerately doped about three orders of magnitude above the Mott density of
\InO{} (about $9\times10^{17}$\cmcu{}), so they can show whether a donor's electron ends up in extended or strongly
localised states, but not hydrogenic binding at device densities. In films thinner than the donor radius (2.7~nm),
quantum confinement can roughly double the binding of shallow donors (more in the strict 2-D limit, and more again if
the surroundings screen less than the film, which the \HfO{}-backed device stack largely prevents), a W-independent
mechanism for thickness-dependent donor activation that this project did not compute. Neutral vacancy energy differences mix local
chemistry with band filling and are not formation energies. Participation ratios with absolute thresholds classify the
same localised support differently in cells of different size; the frozen H-N thresholds correspond to localised
supports of about 6--7 atoms in the bulk and thin-film cells and about 13 atoms in the 2.0~nm cells.
\end{keyresult}
